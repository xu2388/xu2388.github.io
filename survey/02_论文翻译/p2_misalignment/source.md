# Generative AI for misalignment-resistant virtual staining to accelerate histopathology workflows
AUTHORS: Jiabo Ma, Wenqiang Li, Jinbang Li, Ziyi Liu, Linshan Wu, Fengtao Zhou, Li Liang, Ronald Cheong Kin Chan, Terence T. W. Wong, …, Hao Chen

### Abstract

Accurate histopathological diagnosis typically relies on multiple chemical stains, a process that is labor-intensive, tissue-consuming, and environmentally taxing. While virtual staining offers a faster, tissue-conserving alternative, its clinical adoption is hindered by the requirement for perfectly aligned paired data, which is difficult to obtain due to tissue distortion during chemical processing. We present a robust virtual staining framework that mitigates spatial mismatches through a cascaded registration mechanism. By decoupling image generation from spatial alignment, our method enables high-fidelity staining even from imperfectly paired or misaligned datasets without altering existing model architectures. Our approach significantly outperforms state-of-the-art models across five datasets, showing a remarkable 23.8% improvement in image quality for highly misaligned samples. In blinded evaluations, experienced pathologists achieved 52% accuracy in distinguishing virtual from chemical stains, indicating that the two were indistinguishable. This framework simplifies data acquisition and provides a scalable pathway for integrating virtual staining into routine clinical workflows.

### Introduction

Histopathological analysis is a cornerstone of clinical diagnostics, enabling the identification and characterization of disease states based on the microscopic examination of tissue samples<sup>1,2</sup>. Traditionally, this process relies on multiple chemically stained tissue sections, where each stain highlights specific tissue components or structures. For example, hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E) staining, which differentiates cell nuclei from the extracellular tissue matrix, is the most widely used staining technique in histopathology<sup>3</sup>. In contrast, the Periodic Acid-Schiff stain, which specifically targets glycoproteins, is commonly utilized in renal pathology<sup>4</sup>. However, the conventional staining pipeline is resource-intensive, requiring significant time, labor, and the use of chemical reagents that contribute to environmental burdens<sup>5</sup>. These limitations have motivated the development of alternative approaches to streamline histopathological workflows, among which virtual staining has emerged as a promising solution<sup>6,7,8</sup>. By digitally transforming label-free tissue images or one stain into target stain, virtual staining techniques offer the potential to save time, conserve tissue, and reduce dependency on chemical reagents, thereby addressing key challenges in modern diagnostic pathology (Fig. 1a).

[FIGURE local=['figures/fig_1.png']] <strong>Fig. 1: Overview of the virtual staining workflow and performance evaluation.</strong>

Despite these advantages, virtual staining methods are not yet widely adopted in clinical settings due to significant technical challenges. Most existing methods rely on well-aligned paired datasets for training, which are difficult to obtain in practice<sup>9,10</sup>. Chemical staining processes inherently alter tissue structures, making it nearly impossible to achieve precise alignment between unstained and stained tissue sections<sup>11,12</sup>. Furthermore, a single tissue section cannot undergo multiple staining procedures without causing damage or information loss, leaving most available datasets either unpaired or only roughly paired<sup>6</sup>. This misalignment hinders the performance of current virtual staining methods, particularly for applications requiring pixel-level accuracy<sup>6,7,8,9,13,14</sup>. To address the problems caused by image misalignment, the CycleGAN framework<sup>15</sup> has been widely adopted. This approach uses an additional generator and adopts cycle-consistency to train both generators, which usually perform better on unpaired data compared with Pix2pix models<sup>14</sup>. However, the cycle-consistency constraint can cause the model to focus more on image style than content, which may lead to content being lost in the generation process<sup>16,17</sup>. Another strategy focuses on minimizing the influence of misalignment. For instance, the pyramid Pix2pix model<sup>18</sup> incorporates a multi-scale loss function to enhance robustness against structural misalignment in the data. Furthermore, RegGAN<sup>19</sup> incorporates a registration model to align the generated image with the target image. By using the aligned generated image to compute the loss, RegGAN further reduces the impact of misalignment. Ideally, the generated image should align well with the input image, while the registered generated image should closely match the target image. However, in RegGAN, only the latter condition is explicitly enforced because the generator and registration model are tightly coupled. This coupling lacks additional constraints to ensure accurate alignment between the input image and the generated image.

To address these limitations, we propose a robust virtual staining framework, termed DGR (Decoupled Generation and Registration), which decouples the image generation and registration processes. Specifically, we introduce two constraints: one to maintain alignment between the input image and the generated image, and another to ensure alignment between the registered generated image and the noisy ground truth (GT). By decoupling the generator and registration networks, DGR enables effective virtual staining of roughly paired images. We evaluated DGR against state-of-the-art virtual staining methods across five datasets. Experimental results demonstrate that DGR outperforms existing approaches, particularly in cases of severe misalignment. Notably, DGR achieves a 3.4 dB (23.8%) improvement over baseline methods. Furthermore, DGR significantly reduces the reliance on accurately aligned paired images, which is critical for real-world applications of virtual staining in pathology.

### Results

We evaluated our proposed DGR framework across four tasks using five datasets (Fig. 1b). DGR was compared against representative methods spanning four categories: (1) paired image-to-image translation models (e.g., Pix2pix (Isola et al.)<sup>14</sup>); (2) unpaired approaches (e.g., CycleGAN (Zhu et al.)<sup>15</sup>, UNSB (Kim et al.)<sup>20</sup>, StegoGAN (Wu et al.)<sup>21</sup>); (3) pathology-specific virtual staining models (e.g., BCI (Liu et al.)<sup>18</sup>, Her2 (Bai et al.)<sup>13</sup>, ASP (Li et al.)<sup>22</sup>, ST (De et al.)<sup>23</sup>); and (4) misalignment-robust frameworks (e.g., RegGAN (Kong et al.)<sup>19</sup>). DGR demonstrated consistently superior average performance on the five datasets, achieving a Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) of 22.914 dB (+4.4%, Fig. 1c), a Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) of 0.676 (+4.0%, Fig. 1d), and a Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) of 0.180 (+7.7%, Fig. 1e). Evaluated on simulated data, DGR consistently outperformed the baseline across multiple noise levels. Notably, under significant misalignment conditions (±5<sup>∘</sup> rotation, ±10% translation, and ±10% scaling), it achieved a substantial PSNR gain of 3.4 dB (23.8%) (Fig. 1f), highlighting its robustness and suitability for real-world clinical applications where precise image alignment is often unattainable.

#### Virtual H&E staining from label-free autofluorescence images

Autofluorescence (AF) imaging offers label-free, intrinsic tissue contrast but lacks the histological detail and diagnostic interpretability of H&amp;E staining. To bridge this gap, we evaluated the capacity of current methods to synthesize diagnostically plausible H&amp;E images from AF inputs. Unsupervised methods like CycleGAN and StegoGAN exhibited poor fidelity due to the absence of pixel-wise supervision, resulting in low PSNR, SSIM, and high LPIPS (Fig. 2a–c). Supervised models such as Pix2pix and BCI substantially improved performance, underscoring the value of paired training. However, their reliance on assumed pixel-level alignment led to artifacts when applied to real-world, roughly aligned data.

[FIGURE local=['figures/fig_2.png']] <strong>Fig. 2: Quantitative and qualitative comparison of virtual H&amp;E staining methods applied to autofluorescence images.</strong>

To mitigate misalignment, Her2 introduced a multi-scale reconstruction loss, while RegGAN embedded a registration module within the generator. Both strategies enhanced alignment and boosted quantitative scores. DGR, by contrast, explicitly decouples virtual staining (generation) from spatial alignment (registration), enabling the generator to focus exclusively on stain transformation. DGR achieved the highest PSNR (27.80 dB; +5.3% over the next-best method) and SSIM (0.766; +4.8%) on the test set (Supplementary Table 4). It also obtained the best scores on perceptual metrics, LPIPS (0.159) and FID (20.264), and the second-best KID (0.011), only 0.001 higher than the ASP model (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Table 5). Given that lower LPIPS and FID values indicate superior visual fidelity, DGR represents a 15.9% and 12.0% relative reduction in perceptual error compared to the next-best performer.

Qualitatively, DGR’s outputs closely recapitulated ground-truth H&amp;E images in both color accuracy and fine morphological detail (Fig. 2g). Mean absolute error heatmaps revealed substantially lower prediction residuals, and intensity profiles across representative tissue axes showed the strongest concordance with GT, achieving Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.908 and 0.745 (Fig. 2f, top and bottom, respectively). These results demonstrate that explicitly separating alignment from staining not only improves quantitative metrics but also enhances diagnostic plausibility.

#### Translating H&E to PAS-AB

We next evaluated virtual staining of Periodic Acid-Schiff-Alcian Blue (PAS-AB), a specialized histological stain that highlights mucins and basement membranes. Our proposed method, DGR, consistently outperformed others across pixel-level metrics PSNR and SSIM (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Table 6) on both internal and external test sets. Specifically, on the internal test set, DGR achieved a PSNR of 18.677 dB (+1.2%) and an SSIM of 0.547 (+1.0%). On the external test set, DGR demonstrated more pronounced gains, improving PSNR and SSIM by 10.1% and 8.0% over the second-best method, respectively.

[FIGURE local=['figures/fig_3.png']] <strong>Fig. 3: Performance of various models in translating H<em>&amp;</em>E-stained images to virtual PAS-AB-stained images.</strong>

In terms of perceptual similarity, DGR produced predictions that were more aligned with the GT in semantic feature space, achieving the lowest LPIPS scores of 0.168 (−3.4% relative error) and 0.220 (−3.1%) on the internal and external test sets, respectively (Fig. 3c). In contrast, while CycleGAN yielded favorable FID and KID scores (Fig. 3d, e, Supplementary Table 7), its generated images exhibited substantial deviations from the GT in both structure and appearance (Fig. 3g). This discrepancy arises because FID and KID rely on features pretrained on natural images, which differ substantially in distribution from histopathology images<sup>24,25</sup>. To further assess structural fidelity, we compared intensity profiles across representative image regions (Fig. 3f). DGR’s intensity profiles showed the closest alignment with GT, achieving the highest Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.908 and 0.745. Collectively, our quantitative and qualitative analyses demonstrate that DGR is highly effective at translating H&amp;E images into high-fidelity virtual PAS-AB stains.

#### Converting H&E to mIHC

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) is a cutting-edge technique widely used in biomedical research and clinical diagnostics to simultaneously visualize and quantify multiple protein biomarkers within a single tissue section. Translating routinely acquired H&amp;E-stained images into high-quality virtual mIHC images represents a compelling yet highly challenging task, with significant implications for reducing reliance on costly and time-consuming re-staining procedures. In this study, we leveraged a publicly available paired H&amp;E-mIHC dataset to evaluate the performance of DGR in this translation task<sup>26</sup>.

Quantitative evaluation demonstrates that DGR achieves the highest fidelity across pixel- and perceptual-level metrics: PSNR of 27.306 (+5.1%), SSIM of 0.830 (+2.5%), and LPIPS of 0.248 (−3.9% relative error), outperforming all competing methods (Fig. 4a–c, Supplementary Table 8). In terms of distribution-based metrics, Pix2pix attained the lowest FID (33.184), while RegGAN achieved the best KID (0.013) (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Table 9). However, despite its favorable FID score, Pix2pix’s generated images exhibit substantial structural and textural deviations from the GT. In contrast, DGR produces outputs that closely resemble chemically stained mIHC images in both appearance and biomarker localization (Fig. 4g). Intensity profile analysis further confirms that DGR’s predictions align most closely with GT across spatial regions (Fig. 4f).

#### Normalizing staining style of H&E image

To further assess the versatility of DGR in stain-related tasks, we evaluated its performance on H&amp;E stain normalization using a publicly available benchmark dataset<sup>27</sup>. Quantitative results demonstrate that DGR outperforms all competing methods in pixel-level fidelity, achieving a PSNR of 23.823 (+1.7% over RegGAN) and an SSIM of 0.734 (+2.1%) (Fig. 5a, b, Supplementary Table 10). In terms of perceptual similarity, DGR performs comparably to RegGAN, with only a marginal difference of 0.005 in LPIPS (Fig. 5c). Notably, DGR also achieves the best performance on distribution-level metrics, attaining the lowest FID (10.253) and KID (0.007) scores (Fig. 5d, e, Supplementary Table 11). Visual inspection also shows that DGR produces stain-normalized images with more consistent color appearance and structural preservation compared to other approaches (Fig. 5g). Further analysis of intensity profiles demonstrates that DGR’s outputs align most closely with the GT, achieving the highest PCC of 0.982 (Fig. 5f).

#### Blinded human evaluation

Across four distinct stain transformation tasks, DGR consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance, generating images that are both quantitatively accurate and visually realistic. However, high fidelity in automated metrics does not necessarily imply perceptual indistinguishability to expert human observers, particularly to pathologists, for whom visual plausibility is critical. To assess whether DGR’s outputs are perceptually indistinguishable from chemically stained images, we conducted a blinded evaluation with an experienced pathologist. We randomly selected 250 virtually stained H&amp;E images alongside their 250 chemically stained counterparts, and similarly compiled 250 virtually stained and 250 chemically stained PAS-AB image pairs. For each staining type, the 500 images were shuffled and presented in random order. The pathologist was asked to classify each image as “real” (chemically stained) or “virtual” (computationally generated).

For H&amp;E images, the resulting confusion matrix (Fig. 1g) reveals near-chance performance, with an overall accuracy of only 52%. A chi-squared test confirmed no statistically significant difference in the pathologist’s responses between real and virtual images. Similarly, in the PAS-AB task, the pathologist was unable to reliably differentiate between chemically and virtually stained images (Fig. 1h). Additional examples of generated H&amp;E and PAS-AB images are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5, respectively.

Together, these results demonstrate that DGR not only outperforms existing methods across quantitative benchmarks but also produces virtual stains that are perceptually indistinguishable from real, chemically stained images, even to an expert pathologist. This strongly supports the clinical potential of DGR for real-world diagnostic applications where visual authenticity is essential.

#### Downstream task evaluation

To validate the clinical utility of the virtually stained images generated by DGR, we conducted comprehensive downstream task evaluations on two publicly available histopathology datasets: UniToPatho for colorectal polyp classification and dysplasia grading<sup>28</sup>, and GCHTID for gastric cancer tumor microenvironment decomposition<sup>29</sup>. The central hypothesis of this evaluation is that high-fidelity virtual staining should not only produce visually plausible results but also enhance or at least preserve the diagnostic information required by downstream machine learning models. To test this, we adopted a two-stage experimental protocol: (1) train a baseline classifier using only H&amp;E images; and (2) train a multimodal classifier that fuses H&amp;E images with corresponding virtually stained mIHC images generated by each method under comparison.

On UniToPatho dataset, the H&amp;E-only model achieved 0.699 accuracy. When augmented with DGR-generated mIHC images, performance improved to 0.723, a relative gain of 2.4% (Supplementary Table 14). Among all compared methods, DGR was the only one that consistently improved both accuracy and F1-score (Fig. 6a). On GCHTID dataset, which comprises eight histologically distinct tissue types, the H&amp;E-only baseline attained 0.660 accuracy. Integration of DGR-synthesized mIHC images increased accuracy to 0.672 (+1.2%, Supplementary Table 13), again outperforming all competing approaches. While StegoGAN and the ST method also exceeded the baseline, several other methods (e.g., CycleGAN), led to performance degradation, likely due to staining artifacts or loss of diagnostically critical structures (Fig. 6b). Confusion matrices further illustrate that the H&amp;E+DGR’s virtual mIHC reduces misclassification rates across most tissue classes (Fig. 6c–f).

[FIGURE local=['figures/fig_6.png']] <strong>Fig. 6: Downstream tasks evaluation and misalignment resilience of DGR across varying levels of misalignment.</strong>

To further assess structural fidelity at the cellular level, we conducted a nuclei segmentation analysis using Cellpose<sup>30,31</sup>. We used the chemically stained HE2PAS-AB dataset as GT and applied the same segmentation pipeline to virtual stains generated by each method. Segmentation accuracy was quantified using the Dice coefficient (DICE), with higher values indicating better preservation of cellular morphology. DGR achieved the highest overall DICE score of 0.422 (Supplementary Table 12), indicating superior structural consistency with real stains. Qualitatively, DGR’s output exhibits well-defined nuclear boundaries and morphological clarity that closely match GT (Fig. 6k), further confirming its ability to preserve diagnostically relevant tissue architecture.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DGR not only produces virtual stains that are superior in visual and quantitative fidelity but also generates representations that are functionally valuable for real-world diagnostic tasks. When combined with blinded human evaluation showing pathologist-level indistinguishability, the consistent gains in downstream classification performance provide compelling evidence for the clinical applicability of DGR, particularly in settings where mIHC is desired but limited by cost, tissue availability, or workflow constraints.

#### Misalignment resilience

Pixel-level misalignment is a pervasive challenge in real-world virtual staining due to physical sectioning artifacts, tissue deformation, and imperfect slide registration. To evaluate the robustness of DGR under such conditions, we curated a high-quality, spatially aligned dataset of 11,918 H&amp;E-PAS-AB patch pairs (256 × 256 pixels). We then synthetically introduced five increasing levels of misalignment by applying random combinations of rotation, translation, and scaling to the input images (Fig. 6j).

We first evaluated the baseline model<sup>13</sup> and observed a pronounced and consistent degradation in PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS as misalignment severity increased (Fig. 6g–i, Supplementary Table 15), highlighting its sensitivity to spatial discrepancies. RegGAN, which incorporates a registration module, exhibited improved resilience, showing a smaller performance drop under severe misalignment, confirming that explicit alignment helps mitigate this issue. In contrast, DGR consistently outperformed all competing methods across all five misalignment levels. Notably, at the most severe misalignment level (Level 5), DGR achieved relative improvements of 3.7% in PSNR, 8.4% in SSIM, and 10.7% in LPIPS over the second-best method. This robustness stems from DGR’s decoupled architecture, which explicitly separates image generation from spatial alignment, enabling each component to specialize without interference. The effectiveness of this disentanglement is further corroborated by the deformation fields produced by the registration networks, which accurately capture complex tissue distortions (Supplementary Fig. 3).

In summary, DGR’s ability to jointly optimize generation and registration, while keeping the two processes distinct, yields a virtual staining framework that is not only quantitatively superior but also highly resilient to real-world misalignment. This represents a critical advancement toward deploying virtual staining in clinical settings, where perfect spatial correspondence between serial sections is rarely achievable.

### Discussion

Our results demonstrate that explicitly decoupling image generation from spatial registration resolves a fundamental barrier to clinical adoption of virtual staining: robustness to tissue misalignment. While prior methods either require perfectly aligned training pairs<sup>6,13,18,32,33,34</sup> or sacrifice structural fidelity for style transfer<sup>15,35,36</sup>, DGR’s architecture uniquely preserves diagnostic content under real-world distortion. This is evidenced not only by consistent PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS gains across five datasets (Fig. 1c–e) but critically, by pathologist-level indistinguishability in blinded evaluation (Fig. 1g, h). These findings reveal that pixel-level alignment constraints are non-negotiable for diagnostic-grade virtual staining, a principle overlooked in style-focused frameworks like CycleGAN that achieved favorable FID/KID scores yet failed perceptually.

In the case of severe misalignment (Fig. 1f), the PSNR increased by 3.4 dB compared to the baseline model, which quantifies a clinically critical improvement of the DGR on real tissue, as consecutive tissue sections undergo inherent deformation during processing. Unlike RegGAN<sup>19</sup>, which couples registration to generation and optimizes only alignment between the registered output and target stain, DGR enforces dual constraints: (1) alignment between input and generated images to preserve tissue morphology, and (2) alignment between registered output and target stain to ensure color fidelity. This architectural separation explains DGR’s advantages, validated by nuclei segmentation (Dice = 0.422, Fig. 6k) and intensity profile correlations that closely mirror chemical stains. This high-fidelity directly improved diagnostic performance: only images enhanced by our DGR method increased accuracy across all pathology tasks (+2.4% on UniToPatho, +1.2% on GCHTID). In contrast, methods like CycleGAN degraded performance by introducing hallucinated structures. This establishes that virtual staining must prioritize anatomical accuracy over perceptual metrics to retain diagnostic information.

Our work recontextualizes misalignment from a nuisance to an intrinsic property of histopathology workflows. Prior approaches treated it as noise to be minimized via multi-scale losses<sup>18</sup> or embedded registration modules<sup>19</sup>, but DGR proves it requires architectural rethinking. By decoupling generation and registration, DGR achieves consistent performance across alignment conditions (Fig. 1f) without sacrificing cellular detail. The blinded pathologist evaluation further validates that such design choices directly impact clinical trust: near-chance differentiation accuracy (52%) between virtual and chemical stains suggests DGR outputs contain diagnostically equivalent information, a milestone unachieved by prior unpaired methods<sup>20,21</sup>.

While generalization to highly heterogeneous external cohorts warrants further validation, DGR establishes that robust virtual staining is attainable without perfectly paired data. This shifts the field’s focus from impractical data curation toward algorithmic resilience—a necessary step toward replacing resource-intensive chemical workflows. Future work will optimize computational efficiency while extending this decoupling principle to diverse stain transformations.

### Methods

#### Problem setting

To aid in the illustration of our method, we introduce the relevant notation in this section. A misaligned image pair is denoted as (<em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>, <em>y</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>), where <em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub> and <em>y</em><sub><em>n</em></sub> belong to two different modalities and are spatially misaligned. We assume the existence of a well-aligned counterpart \({\widehat{y}}_{n}\) for each <em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>; however, \({\widehat{y}}_{n}\) is not available in real-world scenarios. Our objective is to train a model using the misaligned dataset [(<em>x</em><sub>1</sub>, <em>y</em><sub>1</sub>), . . . , (<em>x</em><sub><em>N</em></sub>, <em>y</em><sub><em>N</em></sub>)], which consists of <em>N</em> pairs, and achieve performance comparable to that of the well-aligned dataset \([({x}_{1},{\widehat{y}}_{1}),...,({x}_{N},{\widehat{y}}_{N})]\).

#### Baseline

The virtual staining methods based on the Pix2pix framework<sup>14</sup> rely on pixel-level <em>L</em><sub>1</sub> loss for optimization, as shown in Eq. (1). However, this framework does not account for misalignment that may be present in the paired images. Therefore, the performance of the framework is heavily dependent on the degree of alignment between the paired images.

$${\min }_{G}{{\mathcal{L}}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | G({x}_{n})-{y}_{n}| {| }_{1}$$

To alleviate the influence of misalignment, Kong et al.<sup>19,37</sup> proposed RegGAN based on the idea of “loss correction"<sup>38</sup> for the magnetic resonance image translation task. This work introduced a registration network (<em>R</em>) to estimate the deformation field <em>ϕ</em> between the generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and the noisy ground truth <em>y</em>, and then use <em>ϕ</em> to re-sample <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) for loss computation. This process can be represented by the following formula:

$${\min }_{G,R}{{\mathcal{L}}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | {\phi }_{n}\circ G({x}_{n})-{y}_{n}| {| }_{1},$$

where ∘ represents a re-sampling operator. In the RegGAN, the authors assumed the noisy ground truth can be expressed as a displacement error, i.e., \(y=\phi \circ \widehat{y}\). However, it is worth noting that Eq. (3) demonstrates that the RegGAN method only guarantees the re-sampled generated image <em>ϕ</em>∘<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) is well-aligned with the noisy ground truth <em>y</em>, and does not explicitly ensure that <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) is well-aligned with the corresponding ideal ground truth \(\widehat{y}\). Since \(\widehat{y}\) is well aligned with the input image <em>x</em>, the misalignment between <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and \(\widehat{y}\) also implies that the generated image may be not well aligned with the input image. Supplementary Fig. 1a illustrates the limitations of solely relying on the registration module (RegGAN<sup>19</sup>) and presents the ideal solution. To address this limitation, we propose an approach that incorporates additional constraints to ensure the alignment between <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and ideal ground truth \(\widehat{y}\).

#### Decoupling generation and registration

Based on the analysis of RegGAN, our findings suggest that the generation and registration processes are inherently interdependent. This occurs because the correction loss function is applied to the re-sampled image <em>ϕ</em>∘<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>), while the generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) itself is not subject to strong constraints such as a correction loss. As a result, the generation and registration stages must be carefully balanced and coordinated to achieve optimal results. Drawing inspiration from GAN models<sup>39</sup>, we introduce an adversarial registration process to decouple generation and registration ensuring that the generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) is accurately aligned with the ideal output \(\widehat{y}\). The framework of our method is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b including registration for noise reduction module and position-consistency generation module for forcing generator to stain input image only without changing the position of pixels.

##### Registration for noise reduction

To reduce the influence of misalignment between <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and <em>y</em>, we first adopt a registration network (<em>R</em><sub>1</sub>) to align the generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) with the “noisy" ground truth <em>y</em>. With the assistance of registration for noise reduction, the reconstruction loss can be represented as following equation:

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{L1}(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | {R}_{1}(G({x}_{n}),{y}_{n})\circ G({x}_{n})-{y}_{n}| {| }_{1},$$

where <em>R</em><sub>1</sub>(<em>G</em>(<em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>), <em>y</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>) is the deformation field between generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and “noisy" ground truth <em>y</em>. Additionally, the structural similarity (SSIM) metric is also adopted to improve the structural similarity between generated images and target images. To use this metric, we utilize following equation:

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{ssim}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}log(1+ssim({R}_{1}(G({x}_{n}),{y}_{n})\circ G({x}_{n}),{y}_{n}))$$

where <em>ssim</em> is function that can calculate the structural similarity between two images.

To prevent overfitting of the deformation field predicted by the registration network <em>R</em><sub>1</sub>, we utilize a smoothness regularization as shown in the following equation:

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{smooth}(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | \nabla {R}_{1}(G({x}_{n}),{y}_{n})| {| }_{2},$$

where ∇ indicates differential operator.To make the model generate realistic images, we also adopt generative adversarial training, the adversarial loss for the generator <em>G</em> and the discriminator <em>D</em> is shown in Eqs. (7) and (8).

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{adv}(x)=-\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}log(D(G({x}_{n})))$$

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{dis}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}\left[log\left(\right.1-D(G({x}_{n}))+log(D({y}_{n}))\right]$$

The total loss for the Registration for Noise Reduction (RNR) is expressed as the following equation.

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{RNR}(x,y)=\alpha {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{L1}(x,y)+\beta {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{ssim}(x,y)+\gamma {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{smooth}(x,y)+\delta {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{adv}(x)$$

##### Position-consistency generation

With the constraint imposed by the registration for noise reduction module, the re-sampled generated image <em>ϕ</em>∘<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and the “noisy" ground truth <em>y</em> are encouraged to be aligned, which improves the credibility of the reconstruction loss (e.g., \({{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{L1}\)). However, a critical limitation persists: there is no explicit constraint ensuring that the generated image itself, <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>), is aligned with the input image, <em>x</em>. This absence of direct constraint can lead to a coupled optimization where the generator <em>G</em> learns to hide structural misalignment in its output, expecting the subsequent registration network <em>R</em><sub>1</sub> to correct it. This coupling contradicts the desired objective of a virtual staining model, which should perform a pure color transformation without altering the spatial position of cellular structures. The intuition behind our approach is that an ideal virtual staining process should be spatially consistent. Since the input image <em>x</em> (e.g., autofluorescence) and the target stain <em>y</em> (e.g., H&amp;E) are acquired from the exact same physical tissue section, they are inherently paired and share an identical underlying biological structure. The primary difference between them is their visual appearance (modality), not their geometry. Therefore, a correctly generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) must constitute a pixel-wise translation of the input <em>x</em> into the target stain’s style. Any spatial misalignment between <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and <em>x</em> is not a property of the data but an artifact of an imperfectly trained generator. Our goal is to enforce this spatial consistency, ensuring the generator learns to change only the stain appearance, analogous to applying a color filter that does not distort the image.

To achieve this goal, we design a constraint as defined in Eq. (10) to optimize <em>R</em><sub>2</sub>.

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T1}(x)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | {R}_{2}({x}_{n},G({x}_{n}))\circ G({x}_{n})-G({x}_{n})| {| }_{1}$$

However, relying solely on this constraint will prevent the registration network <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> from learning informative deformation field. <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> could simply produce an identity deformation field for any given paired images, failing to extract useful information. To prevent <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> from circumventing the learning process, we need an additional constraint that compels <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> to accurately predict the true deformation field.

From Eq. (10), we can see that generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and the input image <em>x</em> will be well aligned. This alignment allows us to conclude that the deformation field between input image <em>x</em> and target image <em>y</em> is identical to the deformation field between generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and target image <em>y</em>. This property can be leveraged to facilitate adversarial training of the registration network <em>R</em><sub>2</sub>. To this end, we utilize the loss function defined in Eq. (11):

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T2}(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | {R}_{2}({x}_{n},{y}_{n})\circ G({x}_{n})-{R}_{1}(G({x}_{n}),{y}_{n})\circ G({x}_{n})| {| }_{1}$$

Here, we aim to minimize the <em>L</em><sub>1</sub> distance between the deformation field of <em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub> and <em>y</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>, and the deformation field of <em>G</em>(<em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>) and <em>y</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>. This loss function encourages registration network <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> to effectively learn how to align cross-modal images (i.e., the input image and its corresponding target image). Additionally, similar to Eq. (6), we also adopt smooth regularization for <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> to avoid overfitting.

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T1R}(x)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | \nabla {R}_{2}({x}_{n},G({x}_{n}))| {| }_{2}$$

$${{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T2R}(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}{\sum }_{n=1}^{N}| | \nabla {R}_{2}({x}_{n},{y}_{n})| {| }_{2}$$

##### Training strategy

The above registration for noise reduction and position-consistency generation modules include generator <em>G</em>, discriminator <em>D</em>, registration networks <em>R</em><sub>1</sub> and <em>R</em><sub>2</sub>. We regard training as two adversarial processes. Firstly, we update the parameters of generator <em>G</em> and registration network <em>R</em><sub>1</sub>, the loss can be represented as:

$${\min }_{G,{R}_{1}}{{\mathcal{L}}}(x,y)={{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{RNR}(x,y)+\epsilon {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T1}(x).$$

Based on this loss, we can infer that the re-sampled image <em>R</em><sub>1</sub>(<em>G</em>(<em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>), <em>y</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>)∘<em>G</em>(<em>x</em><sub><em>n</em></sub>) will be aligned well with the target image <em>y</em>, however, it can not ensure that the input image <em>x</em> can be well aligned with the translated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) due to that <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> could simply output identity mapping for all images to minimize \({{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T1}(x)\). To avoid laziness of <em>R</em><sub>2</sub>, we update the registration network <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> using constraints described in the position-consistency generation module as shown in following equation.

$${\min }_{{R}_{2}}{{\mathcal{L}}}(x,y)={{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T1}(x)+\gamma {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T1R}(x)+{{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T2}(x,y)+\gamma {{{\mathcal{L}}}}_{T2R}(x,y)$$

Finally, we update the discriminator using the loss function defined in Eq. (8).

This training strategy enables us to jointly train the generator and both registration networks, while also ensuring that the generated image is well-aligned with the input image. By incorporating adversarial components into our training process, our model can effectively learn informative features and achieve superior performance.

To gain a clear understanding of proposed DGR model, we intentionally introduced misalignment into the paired data. Then we visualized the generated images and the related registered images (Supplementary Fig. 1c). It is evident that the input image <em>x</em> and the generated image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) are properly aligned, meanwhile the registered image <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>)<sub><em>M</em>1</sub> also shows alignment with the target image <em>y</em>. The decrease of mean absolute error (MAE) of error map ∣<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>)<sub><em>M</em>1</sub> − <em>y</em>∣ compared to ∣<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) − <em>y</em>∣ further supports the efficacy of registration network <em>R</em><sub>1</sub>. To evaluate the effectiveness of <em>R</em><sub>2</sub>, the error maps ∣<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) − <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>)<sub><em>M</em>2</sub>∣ and ∣<em>G</em>(<em>x</em>)<sub><em>M</em>1</sub> − <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>)<sub><em>M</em>3</sub>∣ are also visualized. The MAE between <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) and <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>)<sub><em>M</em>2</sub> demonstrates that the deformation field <em>M</em><sub>2</sub> produced by <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> with input <em>x</em> and <em>G</em>(<em>x</em>) closely resembles an identity mapping, aligning with our intended objective. However, challenges persist for <em>R</em><sub>2</sub> in achieving perfect alignment between input and target images, indicating potential for improvement in its performance.

#### Datasets

To validate the effectiveness of DGR, we adopted five datasets for various virtual staining tasks. The size and number of images are reported in Supplementary Table 1.

##### AF2HE dataset

The AF2HE dataset comprises 15 whole slide images (WSIs) of breast and lung cancer tissue samples<sup>40</sup>. The specimens were initially imaged in their native autofluorescence (AF) state using a widefield microscopy system equipped with a 265 nm ultraviolet excitation source and a 10× objective lens, enabling high-contrast nuclear visualization without staining<sup>41</sup>. Following AF imaging, the same slides were subjected to standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&amp;E) staining and rescanned under a 20× objective lens using a whole-slide scanner. The image pairs were rigorously aligned using the VALIS framework to ensure spatial correspondence<sup>42</sup>. Subsequently, the registered WSIs were partitioned into 128 × 128 patches, resulting in a total of 50,447 paired patches for training and 4422 for testing.

##### HE2PAS-AB dataset

This dataset was collected from clinical specimens at the Prince of Wales Hospital in Hong Kong. It consists of WSIs from which a total of 10,727 aligned patch pairs (128 × 128 pixels) were extracted for training and 1191 for testing. Each tissue section underwent sequential staining: initially with H&amp;E, followed by de-staining via acid alcohol treatment, and finally restaining with PAS-AB. All WSIs were aligned using the VALIS framework to ensure precise spatial registration between staining modalities. To rigorously assess model generalizability across different patient populations within the same institution, an external validation set containing 2841 patches was additionally constructed. These patches were sourced from separate tissue sections and distinct patient cohorts that did not overlap temporally or spatially with the training or test samples, thereby ensuring independent evaluation.

##### HEMIT dataset

The dataset employed in this work is derived from the collection introduced by Bian et al.<sup>26</sup>, consisting of histologically paired H&amp;E and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) whole-slide images originating from the ImmunoAlzer<sup>43</sup>. A key advantage of this dataset is that both staining protocols were applied sequentially on the same tissue section, thereby minimizing structural discrepancies and ensuring high pixel-level alignment—a notable improvement over conventional consecutive-section approaches. The mIHC staining incorporates three biologically relevant markers: DAPI for nuclear visualization, panCK for tumor region delineation, and CD3 for T-cell identification, each corresponding to a distinct channel in the image (blue, red, and green, respectively). We adopt the official partitioning of the data into training, validation, and test sets with 3717, 630, and 945 image pairs, respectively, ensuring that each partition contains tissue samples from different patients to prevent data leakage. To reduce computational demands during model training, we extract a central region of 512 × 512 pixels from each original 1024 × 1024 patch. Additionally, all H&amp;E images undergo stain normalization to enhance consistency and generalization across samples.

##### A2H dataset

The A2H dataset was re-organized by Kang et al.<sup>27</sup>, and the images were obtained from the MITOS_ATYPIA ICPR-14 challenge<sup>44</sup>. Original WSIs were stained with H&amp;E and digitized using two distinct slide scanners (Aperio Scanscope XT and Hamamatsu Nanozoomer 2.0-HT). The processed images have a size of 256 × 256. This dataset consists of 19,200 pairs for training and 7936 pairs for testing. It is used to evaluate the performance of the proposed method in the stain normalization task.

##### Well-aligned HE2PAS-AB dataset

To investigate the resilience of DGR on datasets with varying degrees of misalignment, we extracted paired patches of size 512 × 512 from H&amp;E and PAS-AB WSIs. These patches were then registered, resulting in a training set of 10,727 pairs and a test set of 1191 pairs, each sized 256 × 256 pixels. Through this registration process, the images were aligned to the greatest extent possible. Subsequently, we introduced five levels of misalignment involving rotations, translations, and scalings to the input images. The input and target images were then center-cropped to 128 × 128 pixels for model training. Detailed configurations for these misalignments are provided in Supplementary Table 15.

##### UniToPatho dataset

The UniToPatho dataset is a labeled histopathological dataset designed for colorectal polyp classification and adenoma dysplasia grading, comprising 8699 image patches<sup>28</sup>. It includes the following classes: 545 Hyperplastic Polyps (HP), 950 samples of Normal tissue (NORM), 454 Tubular Adenomas with High-Grade dysplasia (TA.HG), 3618 Tubular Adenomas with Low-Grade dysplasia (TA.LG), 916 Tubulo-Villous Adenomas with High-Grade dysplasia (TVA.HG), and 2186 Tubulo-Villous Adenomas with Low-Grade dysplasia (TVA.LG). For downstream task evaluation, we randomly partitioned the dataset into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:1:2 ratio, respectively.

##### GCHTID dataset

The Gastric Cancer Histopathology Tissue Image Dataset (GCHTID) is a labeled histopathological dataset for gastric cancer tumor microenvironment (TME) decomposition, containing 31,096 non-overlapping 224 × 224 patches extracted from 300 whole slide images of H&amp;E-stained gastric cancer tissues<sup>29</sup>. It includes eight tissue classes: Adipose (ADI), Debris (DEB), Lymphocytes (LYM), Mucus (MUC), Smooth Muscle (MUS), Normal mucosa (NORM), Stroma (STR), and Tumor (TUM), with exactly 3887 patches per class. For downstream evaluation, we randomly split the dataset into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:1:2 ratio.

#### Experiments setting

For the models utilized in this study, we adhered to the hyperparameters recommended in the original papers for training. Our method employs the Adam optimizer<sup>45</sup> for all models. Specifically, for the AF2HE, HE2PAS-AB, and A2H datasets, the learning rate for <em>G</em> is set to 5e−5, while for the remaining HEMIT dataset, the learning rate is decayed to 1e−5. A comprehensive list of the hyperparameters is provided in Supplementary Table 2. All experiments were conducted using PyTorch<sup>46</sup> on a workstation equipped with eight NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs.

##### Computational efficiency

To evaluate the computational efficiency of the proposed method, we compare the training speed, memory consumption, and inference time of various models (Supplementary Table 3). As shown in the results, our approach achieves highly efficient inference since the registration modules are not used during this stage, making it particularly suitable for practical deployment. Even during training, our method remains significantly faster than traditional unsupervised approaches such as CycleGAN, demonstrating the computational advantages of the DGR architecture.

##### Metrics and statistical analysis

To evaluate the generated images, we adopt Peak Signal to Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity (SSIM), Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)<sup>47</sup>, Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)<sup>24</sup>, and Kernel Inception Distance (KID)<sup>25</sup>. PSNR and SSIM quantify pixel-level fidelity between generated and reference images, while LPIPS measures perceptual dissimilarity based on deep feature representations. In contrast, FID and KID assess the similarity between the distributions of generated and real images in feature space, with KID offering an unbiased estimator of distributional divergence. Additionally, we utilized a two-tailed independent samples <em>t</em>-test for statistical analysis unless other methods were explicitly stated.
